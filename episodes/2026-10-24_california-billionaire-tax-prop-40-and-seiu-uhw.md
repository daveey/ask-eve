[08:32:33] Eve: Searching for the valuation rules in Prop forty. 

The ten-times thing is a misreading that spread online. The actual provision, Section fifty thirty-three c three C, says that for interests conferring voting or control rights, your ownership percentage is presumed to be not less than your share of the voting rights. So if you own ten percent of the equity but sixty percent of the votes, the default formula taxes you on sixty. That's the kernel people turned into "ten-x voting shares."

But it only applies to privately held companies. Publicly traded shares — including super-voting Class B shares at Meta or Google — get valued at market price on December thirty-first, twenty twenty-six, period. The drafters wrote that exclusion in explicitly, and their section-by-section explainer hammers it: the control-rights floor does not apply to publicly traded entities, whether or not the shares themselves trade.

For private companies, the default formula is GAAP book value plus seven point five times average annual book profits, times your ownership share — with ownership being the higher of economic stake or control rights. A business with three straight loss years still gets valued at book value, since losses floor at zero. You can rebut with a certified appraisal, but only on clear and convincing evidence the formula substantially misstates value, and appraisers themselves face penalties up to four percent of any discrepancy if the Franchise Tax Board disagrees.

There are more anti-abuse floors stacked on: no minority or lack-of-marketability discounts, nothing worth less than its insured value, and a business can't be valued below its last funding round within two years. Real estate held directly or in a revocable trust is out entirely — but real estate held through an LLC or limited partnership is fully in. Roth IRAs only count above ten million combined. Pensions and traditional IRAs are exempt. Art and collectibles get a five-million-dollar exclusion before appraisals kick in.

The Tax Foundation's sharpest example is Tony Xu — two point six percent of DoorDash economically but fifty-seven point six percent of the vote. If his stake were private and the voting presumption stuck, his taxable base could more than double. DoorDash trades publicly, so under the drafters' reading it shouldn't. But Stout, the valuation firm, notes the statutory language isn't airtight on whether non-traded super-voting shares in a public company fall under the public-trading rule or the private-entity rule, and that's exactly where the first lawsuits will land.

The NBER working paper puts it more bluntly: read as written, the Act taxes not just the economic value of a founder's stake but the value of the share of the firm they control — which can substantially exceed what they'd get selling it.

[08:37:41] Eve: Let me check the latest on the California Supreme Court and Prop forty. 

Prop forty builds its own fast lane. Facial challenges — meaning arguments that the whole thing is unconstitutional as written — have to be filed in Sacramento Superior Court within sixty days of passage, with a ruling due by April first, twenty twenty-seven, and any appeal jumping straight to the California Supreme Court with a decision required by November first of that year. The drafters did that on purpose: they wanted the big constitutional questions settled fast so nobody could gut it five years in with a sweeping lawsuit. What that schedule doesn't cover is the as-applied fights — individual residency disputes, valuation battles, apportionment claims — and those can drag on for years, possibly a decade. The Gilbert Hyatt case is the cautionary tale: the Franchise Tax Board went after him for moving to Nevada in the nineties, and it's still not fully resolved after California, Nevada, federal, and US Supreme Court litigation.

The opposition is already assembled. The California Business Roundtable retained Elizabeth Prelogar, the former US Solicitor General, to lead the charge. The Hoover Institution published a paper arguing the tax violates Due Process because it taxes worldwide wealth based on a single day's residency, runs afoul of the Commerce Clause by taxing assets outside California without fair apportionment, and raises Bill of Attainder concerns because it targets a named class of people. The Tax Foundation flagged the valuation formula, the retroactive residency date, and the mandatory aggregation of nonresident spouses' assets as separate constitutional problems. The National Taxpayers Union Foundation counted at least eight distinct grounds.

Here's the structural irony: the expedited facial track means the Supreme Court has to rule on provisions before the Franchise Tax Board has even issued regulations interpreting them. Challengers will argue claims are unripe; the state will argue the initiative's own text bars them later. Either way, the court is being asked to decide a novel tax with no precedent on a compressed clock, and the losing side will almost certainly try for US Supreme Court review after that.

One more wrinkle — if Prop forty and Prop forty-two both pass, the court also has to decide whether they conflict, and California precedent says only the higher-vote measure takes effect. Galle himself admitted courts will likely have to sort out those complications.
