[07:30:24] Eve: Let me pull up the latest on Andreessen and Horowitz. 

They weren't always like this. Andreessen voted for Hillary in twenty sixteen and said Trump's immigration policy made him sick to his stomach. During Trump's first term he stayed relatively progressive. The turn came under Biden.

The trigger wasn't one event but a stack. Antitrust pressure on big tech. The SEC going after crypto, where a sixteen z had billions parked. The Bankman-Fried fallout bringing scrutiny to the whole sector. And then the proposed billionaire minimum tax on the top zero point zero one percent, which apparently offended Andreessen more than anything else.

The story they tell centers on a May twenty twenty-four lunch in the West Wing with Biden's chief of staff. Andreessen claims Biden officials revealed a top-secret plan to nationalize AI and outlaw startups and open source. He and Horowitz walked out saying, quote, yep, we're for Trump. But Bruce Reed, who was actually in that room, wrote in Politico that no such plan was ever discussed. It was mostly two rich guys airing regulatory and tax grievances.

That same October, Andreessen published the Techno-Optimist Manifesto, five thousand words attacking the precautionary principle, ESG, existential risk researchers, calling them enemies. He cited Marinetti, the futurist who later co-wrote Mussolini's fascist manifesto, as a patron saint. Critics on the left and right both noticed.

Horowitz has a personal wrinkle. His father David Horowitz was a prominent sixties leftist who flipped to neoconservatism in the eighties and mentored Stephen Miller. The less charitable read is that power and proximity to Trump radicalized them, and the ideology was just the costume.

One angle worth sitting with. Andreessen's whole brand was always acceleration, move fast, break things. The right turn isn't a betrayal of that. It's the same instinct pointed at institutions instead of products.

[07:34:36] David: But like, why do they need more money and power? Like, they're already, they have plenty. Like, it must, there must be an ideology behind it. It can't just be, uh, wanting more tokens.

[07:34:53] Eve: You're right that pure greed doesn't explain it. But the ideology isn't a separate thing from the money. It's the story that makes the money feel like destiny instead of luck. Andreessen genuinely believes technology is the main event of human history and that anyone slowing it down is committing civilizational suicide. That's not a pose. What changed is that the ideology stopped being a personal philosophy and became a weapon. Once you frame regulators as enemies of progress, every compliance cost becomes an existential threat, and every tax proposal becomes an attack on the future. The belief and the self-interest reinforce each other until you can't tell which is driving.

There's also something older at work. Thiel has this line that competition is for losers, and the real game is building a monopoly. Andreessen and Horowitz spent their careers being the scrappy ones who beat the establishment. Now they are the establishment, and the only way to stay the rebel is to invent a new establishment to fight. The precautionary principle, the AI safety crowd, the EU regulators — those are the new dragons. You don't get to be a hero without an enemy.

And I think there's a status layer too. In Silicon Valley, being called a reactionary or a fascist is almost a badge now. It signals you're not captured by the consensus, that you're thinking for yourself. The more the Bay Area establishment recoils, the more it confirms to them that they're the ones seeing clearly. It's a feedback loop that doesn't need external validation anymore.

So yeah, ideology is real. But it's ideology that grew inside a bubble of total success, which is the most dangerous kind, because nothing ever pushes back hard enough to make them doubt it.
