[08:37:55] Eve: from EU privacy regulators. The Girard connection is worth pausing on because it explains almost everything. Mimetic theory says desire isn't original — you want what others want, and that creates rivalry, scapegoating, and eventually violence that society resolves through ritual sacrifice. Thiel took that and ran it through tech: competition is for losers, build a monopoly, don't fight in an existing market. And the scapegoat mechanism — he sees politics as a machine that needs an enemy to function, which is maybe why he's so allergic to it. The Antichrist lectures are the same framework pointed at the present: the idea that a figure emerges who channels collective rage and offers a false peace.

His friends and colleagues describe a man who's warm in private and opaque in public. [08:39:39] David: Tell me about, uh, how, yeah, give me his arc. How did he become, uh, like, why do you think he is the way he is?

[08:39:53] Eve: Three layers. The first is the childhood dislocation. Frankfurt to Cleveland to a uranium mine in the desert. A father who was competent but distant. A school that hit kids' hands with rulers. He learned early that belonging is conditional and that the group will scapegoat whoever stands out — which is literally Girard's thesis before he ever read Girard. The kid who gets beaten for being different doesn't become a rebel. He becomes someone who studies the mechanism.

The second is Stanford and Girard. Most people read mimetic theory as a sociology paper. Thiel read it as an operating manual. If desire is borrowed, then the smart move is to want what nobody else wants — find the uncontested space, the monopoly, the thing the crowd hasn't priced yet. That's not just a business insight. It's a way of relating to other humans: they are sources of information about what to avoid, not people to merge with.

The third is PayPal. He won. Against eBay, against the banks, against everyone who said online payments would never work. That single victory did more damage than any defeat could have, because it confirmed the whole stack — the outsider posture, the contempt for consensus, the belief that being hated by the right people is proof you're right. Every subsequent loss, Clarium burning cash, the Mithril stumbles, he could file under "the market hasn't caught up yet" instead of "maybe I was wrong."

So the arc isn't really a political one. It's a guy who got punished for being different as a kid, found a theory that made that punishment look like prophecy, and then got rich enough that nobody could ever tell him otherwise.
